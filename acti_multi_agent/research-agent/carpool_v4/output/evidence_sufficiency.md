## Beat 1: Small-Town Campus Transportation & Coordination Gaps 🟡 adequate

Supporting papers: 21
Key papers MISSING: Direct study of transportation insecurity in small-town or rural university settings, Campus-focused comparison of commercial rideshare coverage versus student mobility demand
Weakness: The corpus can motivate a general campus mobility and coordination problem, but it does not directly establish that commercial ridesharing systematically underserves small-town universities. Category A is broad mobility literature, and Category B adds trust/coordination context rather than direct transport evidence.

Evidence chain:
  → Category A provides recent background on mobility gaps and access problems.
  → Category B adds platform trust and coordination concerns relevant to peer exchange.
  → Together they justify the problem space as motivation, but only for a narrower claim: campus mobility coordination is plausibly underserved, not definitively that rideshare markets fail in small-town campuses.

## Beat 2: Grassroots Coordination via WeChat/WhatsApp and Integrated Campus Platforms 🟠 weak

Supporting papers: 28
Key papers present: Please Call the Specialism: Using WeChat to Support Patient Care in China, Do Not Harm in Private Chat Apps: Ethical Issues for Research on and with WhatsA
Key papers MISSING: Study of Chinese/international students using WeChat or WhatsApp for everyday coordination on U.S. campuses, Empirical design or evaluation paper on integrated campus or 'super-app' platforms
Weakness: The corpus supports a plausible analogy that messaging apps enable grassroots coordination, but most C papers are healthcare, crisis, or solidarity contexts rather than student mobility. D papers address international students, but not strongly their use of messaging platforms for transport coordination. I offers only thin, weakly connected support for integrated campus platforms, so this beat should be framed as an empirically informed inquiry rather than a settled literature-backed claim.

Evidence chain:
  → Category C shows messaging apps can support grassroots coordination in other domains.
  → Category D supports the relevance of international-student populations, but not specifically mobility coordination through WeChat/WhatsApp.
  → Category I gives only sparse support for integrated multi-module campus platforms.
  → Low cross-category connectivity means the beat is suggestive rather than well-assembled.

## Beat 3: Four Design Primitives: Identity Verification, Safety, Rating Fairness, and Rewards 🟡 adequate

Supporting papers: 37
Key papers present: Trust and power in Airbnb’s digital rating and reputation system, Cooperation or competition - When do people contribute more? A field experiment, Taking the Pulse of US College Campuses with Location-Based Anonymous Mobile App
Key papers MISSING: Campus-scoped identity-verified reputation-system design paper, Safety-design study for peer ridesharing in closed university communities
Weakness: There is enough literature to motivate each primitive individually, but the corpus does not strongly integrate them into a single campus ridesharing framework. Identity verification is especially underdeveloped for campus-bounded, institutionally verified settings, and the graph shows almost no synthesis across E/F/G/H.

Evidence chain:
  → E, F, G, and H collectively provide literature for trust, safety, ratings, and incentive design.
  → The Airbnb reputation paper is the clearest anchor for rating-system concerns.
  → Reward/gamification has a recognizable experimental basis, but not in campus carpool settings.
  → The beat works as a design rationale, not as a validated unified theory.

## Beat 4: Primary Formative Survey: Passenger WTP, Motivations, and Driver Supply Willingness 🟡 adequate

Supporting papers: 20
Key papers MISSING: Comparable campus carpool formative study reporting both willingness-to-pay and willingness-to-drive
Weakness: This beat is carried by the paper’s own formative survey, not by the literature corpus. The available literature only contextualizes the results. The evidence is sufficient for local descriptive design input, but not for general population claims given N=44 finished, completion around 40%, and the disclosed Mandarin-heavy respondent skew.

Evidence chain:
  → The direct evidence is the paper’s survey, including the F6 willingness gradient from Q23.
  → F and H offer contextual discussion for safety/reputation and participation design, not proof of the survey findings.
  → Because the beat is descriptive and scoped, the evidence is adequate for informing design decisions in this setting.
  → It should be stated narrowly: local formative evidence for a particular campus population.

## Beat 5: Driver-Subset Tolerance and Rating-Fairness Asymmetry 🟠 weak

Supporting papers: 20
Key papers present: Trust and power in Airbnb’s digital rating and reputation system
Key papers MISSING: The Bias of Crowds: How Gender and Race Stereotypes Influence Customer Ratings of Drivers in the Gig Economy, Empirical study comparing driver and rider tolerance for unfair ratings in mobility platforms
Weakness: This is the most fragile inferential beat. The observed asymmetry is interesting and properly resonant with reputation/algorithmic-management concerns, but the claim can only be made for a narrow subgroup observation from N=19 Driver/Both respondents, with N=12 Rider-only used as methodological justification rather than substantive confirmation. The literature supports why the result matters, not that the pattern should be expected in campus carpools.

Evidence chain:
  → The subgroup result is internally reportable as a formative design finding, not a general behavioral conclusion.
  → H provides relevance for rating fairness, and J helps explain why formalized ratings may create harm or asymmetry.
  → The Airbnb reputation paper is a useful conceptual anchor for power and trust in rating systems.
  → Given the very small subgroup and sparse direct literature fit, the beat should remain explicitly tentative.

## Beat 6: CampusRide Multi-Module Platform and Carpool Deep-Dive 🟡 adequate

Supporting papers: 37
Key papers present: Taking the Pulse of US College Campuses with Location-Based Anonymous Mobile App, Trust and power in Airbnb’s digital rating and reputation system, Cooperation or competition - When do people contribute more? A field experiment
Key papers MISSING: Deployment evaluation of an integrated campus mobility/community platform, Prior artifact paper on institutionally verified student-to-student ridesharing
Weakness: The literature is sufficient to contextualize the design primitives behind the artifact, and the paper appears to have a genuine implemented system. However, the corpus does not provide strong prior art for a comparable integrated campus platform, and there is no deployment evaluation. This beat is sufficient only if framed as design-and-implementation, not effectiveness.

Evidence chain:
  → The artifact is supported by a reasonable set of contextual papers across identity, safety, ratings, and rewards.
  → The campus app paper helps anchor campus-bounded digital interaction design, while reputation and incentive papers justify key module choices.
  → The paper’s strongest move is mapping specific survey findings to specific design decisions.
  → Because no field deployment is evaluated, the beat supports artifact contribution rather than outcome claims.

## Beat 7: Adversarial Scoping 🟡 adequate

Supporting papers: 24
Key papers present: Trust and power in Airbnb’s digital rating and reputation system, Do Not Harm in Private Chat Apps: Ethical Issues for Research on and with WhatsA
Key papers MISSING: Algorithms at Work: The New Contested Terrain of Control, Study of gamification harms in mutual-aid or campus transportation platforms
Weakness: The corpus does give real support for this beat, especially around reputation harms, private-chat ethics, and platform-mediated risks, but most of that literature comes from adjacent platform contexts rather than institutionally verified campus carpools. The scoping claims about sample skew, no deployment evaluation, and .edu-bound applicability rely primarily on the paper’s own methodological honesty rather than external literature.

Evidence chain:
  → J supplies genuine critical literature on platform harms and formalization risks, and H connects directly to rating-fairness concerns.
  → The H-J bridge is one of the strongest links in the corpus, which helps this beat more than most others.
  → The private-chat ethics paper supports caution around informal coordination channels and their study.
  → This beat works because it limits the thesis rather than defending it.

---

## Overall Assessment
The corpus is sufficient for a cautious, scoped paper whose strongest contribution is: a small-town campus coordination problem motivates inquiry; informal messaging-based coordination plausibly exists among international students; four design primitives motivate an implemented campus artifact; and local formative survey data informs the carpool module. The corpus is not sufficient for stronger claims that commercial ridesharing broadly fails in small-town universities, that integrated campus platforms are an established solution class, or that the Beat 5 subgroup effect generalizes. No beat reaches 'strong' because the citation graph is extremely fragmented (100 nodes, 6 edges, 89 isolated), so the paper depends more on careful scoping and honest local evidence than on a tightly cumulative literature chain.
